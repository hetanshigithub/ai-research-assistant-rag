An embedding model maps text to a dense vector so that passages with similar meaning end up close together. Sentence-embedding models such as MiniLM produce vectors of a few hundred dimensions and run comfortably on a CPU, which makes them a practical choice for local retrieval systems.

Embedding quality depends on how well the model's training data matches the documents. A general-purpose model may place two finance passages close together because they share vocabulary, even when they make opposite claims. Domain-specific text, abbreviations, and numbers are common weak spots, so a retrieval system should be evaluated on its own documents rather than on public benchmarks alone.

Most retrieval systems normalize embeddings to unit length so that cosine similarity can be computed as a simple dot product. Queries and documents must be embedded by the same model and with the same settings; mixing models silently produces meaningless similarity scores.

Embedding models are versioned artifacts. When a model is upgraded, every stored vector becomes stale and the index must be rebuilt. Recording the model name and vector dimension next to the index lets a service detect the mismatch at startup instead of returning poor results.
